About this project

How the Korean Women History Project came together, how interviews are recorded, and how to contribute.

About Us

Our mission is to preserve the stories of the Korean women who married American servicemen during and after the Korean War, and to give them their place in history. We record interviews with the women themselves and their relatives, and we collect their photographs, letters and documents into one digital archive. The goal is to move these stories out of private family memory and into public knowledge. We also want to make this an easy way for people to find resources about Korean War Brides and other Korean women. If you or someone in your family has a story to share, we would love to hear from you.

Why Korean War Brides Matter

From the early 1950s onward, an estimated 100,000 Korean women came to the United States as the wives of American servicemen.1 Many arrived speaking little English, in towns where they were the only Korean anyone knew. They met suspicion and hostility from Americans and stigma from other Koreans being labeled as “양공주,” translating into “Western Princess.”2 Still, they raised families, started businesses, and, after immigration law changed in 1965, sponsored parents, brothers and sisters to join them. A large part of the Korean American community today traces its roots to one of these women.2 Yet their lives are rarely recorded, and as this generation grows older, the chance to hear their stories in their own words is running out.

  1. Ji-Yeon Yuh, interview with Crossing East, 2006. crossingeast.org
  2. Ji-Yeon Yuh, Beyond the Shadow of Camptown: Korean Military Brides in America (New York: NYU Press, 2002). nyupress.org

Method

Interviews are recorded in person where possible and remotely where not. Narrators may review their interview before it is published, and may ask for any part of it to be removed. Recordings are published in full rather than edited into a narrative, so that the pacing and digressions of each account are preserved.

A note on language

"War bride" is the term used in the immigration records and press of the period, and it is the phrase most narrators recognize. It is used here for that reason, with an awareness that it was not always a term the women chose for themselves.
