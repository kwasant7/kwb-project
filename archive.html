Exclusion, 1879-1924

For decades before the Korean War, American law kept Asian immigrants out. By 1924 almost no one from Asia could immigrate, and that included the Korean wives of American servicemen.

The war bride laws, 1945-1965

After World War II, Congress began letting servicemen bring their foreign wives home. For Asian wives each opening came separately, until the 1965 act ended the quota system and made family the center of immigration law.

Meeting in Korea

Most of the women met their husbands near U.S. bases in South Korea, during the war or after it. Many lived in or near the camptowns (기지촌, kijichon) that grew up around the bases, and the camptowns’ reputation was pinned on all of them, whether or not they had lived there.

Arriving in America

When Yong Soon “Blue” Morgan, billed as the first Korean war bride, landed in Seattle on October 18, 1951, newspapers across the country ran the story. Most of the women who came after her got no coverage at all.

After the welcome

Within a few years the coverage changed. Korean war brides still made the papers, but more often when something had gone wrong at home: a quarrel that ended with a gun, a fight over a child, a mother left alone with her daughters.

The Korean Women’s Association

The Korean Women’s Association (KWA) began in 1972, when Hongja White started gathering Korean military wives near Fort Lewis in her Tacoma home and feeding them. Many were struggling with culture shock, language barriers, and abuse. By 1978 the association was running English and citizenship classes, counseling, job placement, and interpretation. It became a nonprofit in 1979 and today runs social services across Western Washington. Park Myung Rae, chair of its board, tells the association’s story in her oral history.

Korean Tacoma

As Korean wives sponsored their families after 1965, a Korean business district grew up along South Tacoma Way in Tacoma and Lakewood. These clippings come from the Tacoma Public Library’s files on the city’s Korean community.
