Exclusion, 1879–1924

For decades before the Korean War, American law kept Asian immigrants out. By 1924 almost no one from Asia could immigrate, and that included the Korean wives of American servicemen.

The war bride laws, 1945–1965

After World War II, Congress began letting servicemen bring their foreign wives home. For Asian wives each opening came separately, until the 1965 act ended the quota system and made family the center of immigration law.

Camptowns in Korea

Many of the women met their husbands near U.S. bases, in the camptowns (기지촌, kijichon) that grew up around them. The stigma attached to those towns followed every one of them, whatever her own story.

Arriving in America

The first Korean wife to land in Seattle made the newspapers. Most of the women who followed arrived with no one watching, and some were left on their own.

The Korean Women’s Association

In 1972, Korean military wives in Pierce County, Washington, began meeting to help one another with isolation, language, and abuse. Their group became the Korean Women’s Association (KWA), which now runs social services across Western Washington.

Documents & further reading

  • Beyond the Shadow of Camptown: Korean Military Brides in America Ji-Yeon Yuh, New York University Press, 2002

    The essential history of Korean military brides, built on interviews with the women themselves. Chapter 3, “Immigrant Encounters,” covers their first years in America.

  • Dr. Ji-Yeon Yuh interview Crossing East, 2006

    Yuh on how an estimated 100,000 Korean women came to the United States as military wives.

  • America’s First Korean War Bride Comes Home TIME, 2014

    Wayne Miller’s 1951 photographs of Blue Morgan’s arrival in Seattle and her first days with her husband’s family.

  • KWA: 50 Years of Helping the Community Northwest Room, Tacoma Public Library

    Photographs of KWA members and the association’s early community work.
