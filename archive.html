Exclusion, 1879-1924

For decades before the Korean War, American law kept Asian immigrants out. By 1924 almost no one from Asia could immigrate, and that included the Korean wives of American servicemen.

The war bride laws, 1945-1965

After World War II, Congress began letting servicemen bring their foreign wives home. For Asian wives each opening came separately, until the 1965 act ended the quota system and made family the center of immigration law.

Meeting in Korea

Most of the women met their husbands near U.S. bases in South Korea, during the war or after it. Many lived in or near the camptowns (기지촌, kijichon) that grew up around the bases, and the stigma attached to those towns followed every one of them, whatever her own story.

Arriving in America

When Yong Soon “Blue” Morgan, billed as the first Korean war bride, landed in Seattle on October 18, 1951, newspapers across the country ran the story. Most of the women who followed arrived with no one watching.

After the welcome

Within a few years the coverage changed. Korean war brides still made the papers, but more often when something had gone wrong at home: a quarrel that ended with a gun, a fight over a child, a mother left alone with her daughters.

The Korean Women’s Association

The Korean Women’s Association (KWA) began in 1972, when Hongja White started gathering Korean military wives near Fort Lewis in her Tacoma home and feeding them. Many were struggling with culture shock, language barriers, and abuse. By 1978 the association was running English and citizenship classes, counseling, job placement, and interpretation. It became a nonprofit in 1979 and today runs social services across Western Washington.

Korean Tacoma

As Korean wives sponsored their families after 1965, a Korean business district grew up along South Tacoma Way in Tacoma and Lakewood. These clippings come from the Tacoma Public Library’s files on the city’s Korean community.

Documents & further reading

Books and articles

  • Beyond the Shadow of Camptown: Korean Military Brides in America Ji-Yeon Yuh, New York University Press, 2002

    The essential history of Korean military brides, built on interviews with the women themselves. Chapter 3, “Immigrant Encounters,” covers their first years in America.

  • Diaspora of Camptown: The Forgotten War’s Monstrous Family Grace M. Cho, Women’s Studies Quarterly 34, no. 1/2 (2006): 309-331

    The daughter of a Korean military bride on how the stigma of the camptowns, and silence about the war, shaped Korean diaspora families.

  • Korean Military Brides in New York Amy Lee and Joseph Tse-Hei Lee, Inter-Asia Cultural Studies 8, no. 3 (2007): 458-465

    The life stories of five Korean military brides in Newburgh, New York, from Korea to their marriages in America.

  • Vulnerability, Resilience and Well-being of Intermarriage: An Ethnographic Approach to Korean Women Jang-ae Yang and Kyoung-ho Shin, Journal of International Women’s Studies 10, no. 2 (2008)

    Interviews with Korean women who married U.S. servicemen, on inequality in their marriages and how they recovered from crises such as divorce.

  • The Pattern of American Society: Treatment of Korean War Brides in the United States Following the Korean War Tatiana Moore, Undergraduate Research Journal (University of Nebraska at Kearney) 24 (2020)

    Compares the treatment of Korean war brides with that of other immigrant and minority groups in postwar America.

  • Red Lights, White Hope: Race, Gender, and U.S. Camptown Prostitution in South Korea Julie Kim, senior thesis, Claremont McKenna College, 2017

    How race and gender were negotiated in the camptowns around U.S. bases, and the stereotype that followed Korean military brides to America.

  • Dr. Ji-Yeon Yuh interview Crossing East, 2006

    Yuh on how an estimated 100,000 Korean women came to the United States as military wives.

  • America’s First Korean War Bride Comes Home TIME, 2014

    Wayne Miller’s 1951 photographs of Blue Morgan’s arrival in Seattle and her first days with her husband’s family.

  • Yoo-sun and Ralph (유선과 랄프) Charley Smith, Positive Thinking (좋은생각), Seoul, 2026

    A son’s book about his parents, Park Yoo-sun and Ralph Smith, who met during the war. The Korea Times wrote about the book and the family in September 2026, and his oral history is part of this project.

Newspaper coverage

  • “New Horizons: Pretty Yong Soon Morgan, 23, First Korean War Bride to Arrive in the U.S. Through Seattle” Progress-Bulletin, October 19, 1951. Newspapers.com

    Another paper’s report on Blue Morgan the same day, one of many that ran the story.

  • “Creating a Legacy of Kindness” Kathleen Merryman, The News Tribune (Tacoma). Newspapers.com

    A profile of departing KWA executive director Lua Pritchard that recounts how the association began in 1972.

Collections

  • Sulja Warnick Papers Community Archives Center, Tacoma Public Library

    An oral history with KWA founding member Sulja Warnick, with photographs, newspaper clippings, and letters about her life and the association’s work.

  • KWA: 50 Years of Helping the Community Northwest Room, Tacoma Public Library

    Photographs of KWA members and the association’s early community work.
